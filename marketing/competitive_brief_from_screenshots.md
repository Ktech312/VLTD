# VLTD Competitive Brief
## Source: 61 App Screenshots Collected May 31 – Aug 6, 2026
### What They Do Well. What We Do Better.

---

> **How to read this:** Every competitor was pulled from real ads and screenshots you collected in the wild — meaning these are the products spending money to reach your users right now. For each one: steal the good, fix the bad.

---

## THE LANDSCAPE IN ONE SENTENCE

Every competitor is either a **single-category tool** (cards, vinyl, comics, art) trying to go deep, or a **general-purpose organizer** trying to go broad. Nobody has built the thing that goes both deep *and* across every collecting universe. That's the gap VLTD fills.

## COMPETITOR ANALYSES

---

### 1. cardpredictai
**Category:** AI sports card price prediction

**What they do well — steal this:**
- The "AI predicts value" hook is compelling and cuts through noise. People are scared of buying wrong. Leading with prediction/intelligence rather than organization is smart positioning — it speaks to the wallet, not the filing cabinet.
- Simple, single-feature promise. No confusion about what the product does.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Sports cards only. If you collect anything else, you don't exist to them.
- Prediction without context is hollow. You can tell me a card is worth $400, but if I can't see where it sits in my portfolio, track its history, or decide what to do next, the prediction is just a number.
- No community, no collection management, no growth path. It's a calculator, not a tool.

**VLTD answer:** Build price intelligence into every category. Your analytics layer covers cards, toys, comics, sneakers, vinyl — all of it. The prediction is table stakes; the portfolio context makes it actionable.

### 2. Toyzie AI
**Category:** Toy/Funko value scanner (100K+ downloads, 4.4★)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- 100K downloads and 4.4 stars means the scan-to-value UX genuinely works. People will take out their phone and point it at something. That's a real behavior.
- AI scanning removes friction from the "how much is this worth?" question. Instant gratification.
- Funko/toy collector community is massive and underserved by serious apps.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- One category. If you collect Funko *and* comics *and* sneakers, you need three separate apps.
- No collection building — it's a lookup tool, not a vault. There's no sense of accumulation, growth, or ownership.
- No analytics, no goals, no portfolio view. You scanned it. Now what?

**VLTD answer:** VLTD should scan too. But when you scan a Funko, it drops into your Vault alongside your comics and your cards. The scan is just the first step. Everything after that is where VLTD wins.

### 3. My Vinyl Vault Online
**Category:** Vinyl record catalog, public beta

**What they do well — steal this:**
- The name "Vault" is doing heavy lifting — it signals permanence, seriousness, and ownership. Collectors respond to it.
- Vinyl collectors are passionate, organized, and willing to pay. This niche has real engagement.
- Public beta approach builds community buy-in before launch.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Vinyl only. One of the most fragmented collecting niches in the world, and they only serve one slice.
- Still in public beta — feature gaps are visible and community is small.
- No pricing layer, no analytics, no cross-category anything.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD already has a Vinyl universe. You're not their niche competitor — you're what a vinyl collector graduates to when they also collect records *and* other things. And you're not in beta.

---

### 4. Dead Format
**Category:** Vinyl + social community (marketplace, forums, archive)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Community-first design. Forums, social feeds, and marketplace integrated around the collection — not bolted on as an afterthought. The community IS the product.
- "Archive" mentality resonates. Collectors care about preservation, documentation, and history — not just current value.
- Organic community building. The forums create retention that pure utility apps can't match.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Vinyl only — again. The community energy doesn't transfer to any other category.
- Dated UI. The app looks like it was built in 2015 and hasn't been redesigned. Trust erodes when the interface feels old.
- Community is fragmented from the actual collection tool. The forums are a separate thing from cataloging.

**VLTD answer:** Build community into the collection experience, not around it. When someone adds a rare pressing to their Vault, that's a social moment. VLTD's community layer should live inside the same view as the collection, not in a separate tab. And the design should feel like a vault — premium, not dated.

### 5. Cardo (getcardo.com)
**Category:** Sports card market intelligence

**What they do well — steal this:**
- "Market intelligence" positioning is more sophisticated than "price checker." It targets serious collectors who think of their collection as an investment.
- Data-forward aesthetic signals credibility. Charts, comps, historical pricing — it looks like a Bloomberg terminal for cards.
- Clean, professional web/app design. Doesn't look like a hobbyist project.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Sports cards only. The market intelligence angle is powerful but completely siloed.
- No portfolio tracking — it tells you what the market is doing, not what your specific collection is worth.
- No community, no social layer, no discovery.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD's analytics tab should match Cardo's sophistication — but across every category. Your portfolio view should feel like a financial dashboard, not a list. Total value, appreciation over time, category breakdown. Cardo proves collectors want this. VLTD delivers it for every universe.

### 6. MintSplice
**Category:** Real-time TCG card pricing overlay

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Real-time pricing is a genuine pain point. Prices move, and collectors need current data when making decisions.
- The overlay concept shows creative thinking about where in the workflow value lives — at the point of transaction, not after.
- TCG community (Magic, Pokémon, etc.) is hyper price-sensitive and data-hungry.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- A pricing overlay is a workaround, not a solution. It solves one moment in the collector journey and ignores everything else.
- TCG only. The problem of needing real-time pricing exists in every category.
- No collection management, no portfolio context, no history.

**VLTD answer:** Real-time pricing should be table stakes, not a separate product. When you're looking at a card in your VLTD vault, current market price should be right there. MintSplice is solving a real problem with a tool instead of a platform. VLTD is the platform.

### 7. Tilt Rips
**Category:** Card ripping/buying/selling, 0% fees

**What they do well — steal this:**
- 0% fees is an extremely strong hook in a space where every platform takes a cut. Leads with a clear financial advantage.
- The "rip" concept (buying sealed packs and revealing cards live) taps into the entertainment/gambling psychology of collecting — the thrill, not just the asset.
- Transaction-focused rather than organization-focused shows a different angle into the same audience.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Pure commerce. No vault, no portfolio, no sense of what you've built. You buy stuff and sell stuff. The collection itself is invisible.
- 0% fees is a race to the bottom — a business model problem, not a product advantage.
- No data, no analytics, no intelligence. You're transacting blind.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD is for after the rip. Once you've got the card, it goes in your Vault. You see what it's worth, where it fits in your portfolio, whether you should hold or trade. Tilt Rips serves the thrill moment. VLTD serves everything after.

### 8. Card Pilot
**Category:** Card collection portfolio tracker

**What they do well — steal this:**
- "Portfolio" framing positions the collection as an asset class, not a hobby. This is the right mental model for serious collectors.
- Tracker-first design — the whole product is built around understanding what you have and what it's worth.
- Financial metaphors (portfolio, value tracking) attract a more serious, spending-capable collector demographic.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Cards only — the portfolio framing should work for every category but they've limited themselves.
- Basic UX. The financial framing raises expectations for polish that the design doesn't deliver.
- No discovery, no community, no goals — pure utility with no aspirational layer.

**VLTD answer:** Card Pilot has the right mental model but executes it in one lane. VLTD delivers the portfolio view across Vinyl, Comics, Sneakers, Cards, Toys — every universe. And the design should match the financial seriousness of the concept. Your analytics and portfolio features should feel premium, not like a spreadsheet alternative.

### 9. My Card Post
**Category:** Collector marketplace, 22K+ members

**What they do well — steal this:**
- 22K members is meaningful social proof. Community at that scale creates network effects — more buyers means more sellers means better prices for everyone.
- Marketplace + community combo keeps people coming back for reasons beyond utility.
- Member count front-and-center as marketing — social proof in the acquisition funnel.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Just a marketplace. No one knows what's in their collection, what it's worth, or how it's grown. The collection itself is invisible.
- Single category (cards). 22K card collectors is a ceiling, not a floor.
- Discovery is transaction-driven, not interest-driven. You find things to buy, not things to admire or track.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD's community is built around the vault, not around transactions. You discover other collectors' collections — what they have, what they love — not just what they're selling. Community should be inspired by collecting, not driven by commerce.

### 10. ThingsBook
**Category:** General collection tracker (films, shows, media — not collectibles)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- "Track anything" positioning has mass appeal — it removes the category constraint entirely.
- Clean, minimal UI. No clutter. The tracking experience itself is the product.
- Check-in behavior (watching, reading, playing) creates daily engagement and habit loops.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Tracks cultural consumption, not physical collectibles. Films you've seen are not the same as items you own. No pricing, no value, no portfolio.
- "Track anything" is so broad it's almost meaningless for serious collectors.
- No sense of pride of ownership — it's a checklist, not a vault.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD tracks what you *own*, not what you've experienced. The difference is ownership — permanence, value, identity. ThingsBook is a diary. VLTD is a vault. Both track things; only one treats your collection as an asset worth managing.

### 11. Blue Dots
**Category:** Home inventory/storage organizer (early stage)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Storage and location tracking is a genuine collector pain point. Where is the thing? Which box? Which shelf? This is real friction.
- Home inventory framing positions the app as practical, not aspirational — appeals to the organized-but-overwhelmed collector.
- Simple onboarding approach. Low barrier to getting started.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Very early stage — limited features, small user base.
- No pricing layer, no analytics, no community.
- "Home inventory" is too functional and not aspirational enough. Collectors don't want to feel like they're managing stuff — they want to feel like they're managing a collection.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD should include location/storage tracking within the vault view. Where is this item? Is it displayed, in storage, on loan? That metadata matters. But VLTD frames it as part of managing your collection, not managing your closet.

### 12. Collectible Lane
**Category:** Multi-category app (Video Games, Trading Cards, Comics, Sports)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Multi-category is the right idea. Video Games + Cards + Comics + Sports in one app is the closest any competitor comes to VLTD's scope.
- Category breadth proves the market exists — collectors do own across multiple universes and want one place.
- Grounding it in the major collectible categories shows market research awareness.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Execution doesn't match ambition. The UI looks generic, the feature depth is shallow, and there's no design sensibility that communicates premium or trust.
- Still misses major categories — vinyl, sneakers, toys, art, watches, memorabilia.
- No strong analytics, no portfolio view, no community. It's a list with categories.
- No clear positioning. "We have multiple categories" is not a brand.

**VLTD answer:** Collectible Lane validates the multi-category thesis. VLTD needs to beat it on every dimension: more categories, deeper features per category (analytics, goals, portfolio), better design, and a clear brand identity. "We have categories" is a feature. "Your vault for everything you collect" is a brand.

### 13. artscapy
**Category:** Art investment platform (UK, FCA-regulated)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Investment platform framing elevates art from hobby to asset class. If you're FCA-regulated, you're serious — and collectors feel that seriousness.
- Portfolio analytics for art: appreciation tracking, market comparables, investment intelligence. This is what serious collectors want from every category.
- Premium, trust-forward design. The product looks like it belongs in the same conversation as wealth management.
- Regulatory credibility (FCA) as a moat — "we're legit" is powerful when you're managing valuable assets.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Art-only, and positioned at high-net-worth investors — not accessible to the everyday collector.
- High minimum investment framing excludes the growing majority of collectors who have valuable collections but aren't institutional.
- No community, no discovery, no sharing — it's a private wealth tool, not a collector platform.

**VLTD answer:** Borrow artscapy's investment intelligence and apply it across every category. Every serious collector — cards, sneakers, vinyl, comics — deserves the same analytical rigor that art investors get. VLTD is the democratic version of artscapy: portfolio-grade intelligence for every collecting universe, not just art, and not just for the wealthy.

### 14. ComicCollx
**Category:** Comics grading newsletter/guide

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Educational content builds authority. If you teach collectors how grading works, they trust your platform with their collection.
- Niche community authority — being the go-to resource for a specific topic creates organic reach.
- Comics grading is a genuine knowledge gap that collectors need filled.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- It's a newsletter, not an app. There's no product — just content.
- No actual collection management, no pricing, no analytics.
- Content authority without a product to convert to is a lot of work for limited retention.

**VLTD answer:** Build education into the platform. When a collector adds their first graded comic, VLTD can surface what the grade means, how it affects value, what to look for. Educational moments inside the product create the same authority as a newsletter — but they also keep users in VLTD, not in their inbox.

### 15. Stadium Insurance
**Category:** Collection insurance

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Insurance is a massive unmet need for serious collectors. If your collection is worth $10K+, you want it protected — and most homeowner's policies won't cover it properly.
- Solves a real pain that most collection apps completely ignore. Pure practical value.
- Positions around protection and security — different emotional register from value/analytics.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Single-service. It's not a collection app at all.
- Requires manual documentation to get insured — a separate, painful process.
- No integration with any collection platform.

**VLTD answer:** VLTD's vault should be the documentation layer for insurance. When you've cataloged your collection in VLTD — items, values, photos — that data *is* your insurance claim. Partner with or surface insurance options directly in the app. "Your vault is your insurance record" is a powerful message that no competitor owns.

### 16. Raremarq (raremarq.com)
**Category:** CGC certification auto-import

**What they do well — steal this:**
- One-click import of CGC certification data is genuinely magical for graded collectors. Nobody wants to manually enter cert numbers, grades, and values.
- Automation of the most tedious part of cataloging (manual data entry) removes the biggest barrier to actually using a collection app.
- Focused on a real collector workflow — the cert number exists, the data exists, just connect them.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- It's one import tool, not a platform. CGC only, no collection management beyond the import.
- No analytics, no community, no vault — just a bridge to data that doesn't go anywhere.
- Limited to graded collectibles — a slice of the market.

**VLTD answer:** Auto-import should be a core VLTD feature. CGC cert import, PSA population reports, eBay sold history, discogs catalog — every data source that already has the information should pipe directly into VLTD. Raremarq solves the problem for one source. VLTD should solve it for every source.

### 17. ETR
**Category:** MTG card portfolio tracking website

**What they do well — steal this:**
- Portfolio tracking with real pricing for MTG cards — treats the card collection as a financial portfolio, tracks price movements over time.
- Website format means desktop-accessible, which MTG players (who often play at tables, not phones) actually prefer.
- Data depth: card-level price history, portfolio-level value tracking, format-specific metrics.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- MTG only. The portfolio model works for every collectible — they just haven't applied it beyond one game.
- Website, not app — limits mobile use and push notifications for price alerts.
- No community, no sharing, no discovery beyond the cards themselves.

**VLTD answer:** ETR proves that collectors want financial-grade portfolio tracking. VLTD delivers this across every category — cards, coins, sneakers, art — with an app-first experience, price alerts, and sharing built in.

### 18. Scanlily
**Category:** AI photo organizer ("Organize Everything")

**What they do well — steal this:**
- "LOST IN YOUR OWN COLLECTION CHAOS?" is brilliant pain-point marketing. It names the exact feeling every overwhelmed collector has. Hook with the pain, not the feature.
- AI photo-to-searchable-database is frictionless onboarding — point your phone at your stuff and it handles the rest.
- "Organize Everything" is broad enough to be universal but specific enough to feel useful.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Not built for collectibles. It organizes your home, your pantry, your office — not your collection.
- No pricing, no value tracking, no analytics. It knows you *have* things; it doesn't know what they're worth.
- No community, no sharing, no pride of ownership. It's a storage system, not a vault.

**VLTD answer:** Borrow the pain-point marketing. "Lost in your own collection chaos?" is something VLTD can answer directly. But VLTD doesn't just organize — it values, tracks, and surfaces what your collection actually means. Scanlily organizes your closet. VLTD manages your vault.

### 19. Panini Direct
**Category:** Official Panini app (licensed manufacturer)

**What they do well — steal this:**
- First-party trust is enormous. When the manufacturer makes the app, collectors feel safe — real products, real data, no fakes.
- Exclusive content and drops are available only through the official app — creates urgency and daily engagement.
- Integration between product catalog and collector account is seamless because they own both ends.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Panini-only. Everything is locked to one manufacturer's universe.
- Proprietary lock-in cuts both ways — great if you only collect Panini, useless if you collect anything else.
- No cross-category portfolio, no analytics, no community beyond Panini's ecosystem.

**VLTD answer:** The first-party trust is a real advantage VLTD can't replicate — but it's also a ceiling. Panini collectors who also collect cards, comics, or sneakers hit that ceiling immediately. VLTD is where the multi-category collector goes when no single manufacturer's app can hold their whole vault.

### 20. VeVe Digital Collectibles
**Category:** Digital collectibles / NFT platform

**What they do well — steal this:**
- "Track your Collecting Journey" is smart messaging — it frames collecting as an ongoing story, not a static inventory. Journey implies growth, discovery, and narrative.
- Polished, premium app design. High-production screenshots and branded colors communicate trust.
- Digital marketplace integrated with the collection — the buying and the owning live in the same place.

**What they do poorly — do this better:**
- Digital only. The physical collector market is orders of magnitude larger than the NFT market, and physical collectors have far fewer tools.
- NFT market volatility has eroded trust in digital-first platforms. The "collecting journey" framing is used but the underlying asset class is speculative.
- No physical collection management, no cross-category, no real-world value integration.

**VLTD answer:** Steal "collecting journey" energy — that sense that your collection is a living story, not a static list. But apply it to physical collections. Your vault grows, evolves, and tells the story of who you are as a collector. VeVe owns the digital collecting journey. VLTD owns the physical one.

## THE ONE PARAGRAPH THAT DEFINES VLTD'S POSITION

Every competitor is either deep in one category or thin across many. They're either tools (price checkers, organizers, import utilities) or communities (forums, marketplaces) — but not both. None of them treat the collection itself as the thing of value. VLTD is the first platform where your entire collection — across every universe you collect in — lives in one vault, grows as a portfolio, and becomes a story you can share. Not a spreadsheet. Not a marketplace. A vault.
